% ===== 2. The paper, as I read it =====
\begin{frame}{The paper, as I read it}
  \vfill
  \card{\small
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.8ex}
    \item \lead{What they do:} put German open-end real estate funds (OREFs)
      into a standard lifecycle savings-and-portfolio model. As far as I can
      tell this is the first time these funds have been placed in such a model.
    \item \lead{How they do it:} illiquidity is built into the model rather
      than added as a cost. Fund holdings are tracked as a separate state
      variable, and the 12-month notice period, ${\sim}5\%$ front-end load, and
      German taxes are all inside the optimization.
    \item \lead{Main finding:} OREFs barely feature during working life but
      become the largest holding in retirement (${\sim}60\%$ of the portfolio
      at the very end). The authors attribute this to the bequest motive, the
      funds' very low volatility, and a tax advantage.
    \item \lead{What I found convincing:} preference parameters are fitted only
      to survey wealth and stock shares, yet the model then lines up with fund
      holdings and investor ages in a separate 1.4\,M-investor dataset. If I
      understand correctly, that is an out-of-sample check.
  \end{itemize}}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

  \bottomrule

  \card{\footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.7ex}
     \item The 5\% load pushes the other
        way; my rough sums suggest you need something like a 15--17 year horizon
        to work it off, which a bequest motive would extend. And the 0.5\% fee on
        bonds is doing a fair amount of the work --- at 0.2\% the gap looks like
        it closes.
      \item I wasn't sure whether the $\varphi$ fee is deductible from the
        taxable base, or why 0.5\% rather than a lower modern figure --- could
        you clarify?
      \item \lead{On the smoothed returns.} You argue the smoothed NAV is the
        price investors actually trade at, which makes sense to me. But the
        latent-risk section shows much higher volatility and discounts in stress.
        Should I think of these funds as genuinely low-risk, or as low-risk most
        of the time with occasional sharp episodes?
  \end{itemize}}
  \vfill

% ===== 5. Questions I'd like to ask (1) =====

% ===== 6. Questions I'd like to ask (2) + close =====
\begin{frame}{Questions I'd like to ask}
  \vfill
  \card{\footnotesize
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.7ex}\setcounter{enumi}{3}
    \item How were the states discretized in the simulations
    \item \lead{The notice period.} Could you say a little more about how the
      one-year notice period changes behaviour? I was interested how the facty that a
      household selling still carries fund risk for a further year influences behavior.
    \item \lead{Acting on predictability.} In the AR predictability model  version,
      households keep buying after good returns even very late in life. Is that
      something investors could/would actually act on, and are the loads and holding
      periods partly there to prevent it?
    \item \lead{How much is specific to Germany?} Germany has both the tax
      exemption and these specific liquidity rules. Do you have a sense of what
      the model would predict where those features are absent --- which of your
      results would you most expect to travel? Europe? USA?
  \end{enumerate}}
  \vspace{1.0ex}
  
  \vfill
\end{frame}
