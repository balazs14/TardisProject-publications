\title{\textbf{Absolute Market Unfairness and the Compression of Bid--Ask Spreads in Cryptocurrency Option Markets}}

\author{Bal\'azs Kr\'alik\thanks{Corresponding Author. Assistant Professor at Corvinus University of Budapest, Institute of Finance, F\H{o}v\'am square 8. Budapest, Hungary, 1093. E-mail: balazs.kralik@uni-corvinus.hu.}, N\'ora Felf\"oldi-Sz\H{u}cs\thanks{Associate Professor at Corvinus University of Budapest, Institute of Finance.}, Tam\'as Rutai\thanks{Student at Corvinus University of Budapest.}, Kata V\'aradi\thanks{Associate Professor at Corvinus University of Budapest, Institute of Finance.}}
\date{}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Put--call parity ties a European call, a put, and a matching future into a one-to-one correspondence, so on a single venue their standing quotes should be mutually consistent up to transaction costs. We propose \emph{Absolute Market Unfairness} (AMU): the part of the quoted put--call-parity violation that remains, at the top of the book, after netting out direct transaction costs. Unlike the put--call-parity literature, which tests \emph{trade} prices, AMU is a statement about \emph{quotes}. It is a model-free, incontrovertible symptom of poor liquidity provision---comparable in basis points across venues, underlyings, and maturities---in the spirit of the best-execution (NBBO) rules that police quote quality where identical instruments trade frictionlessly on several venues. It does not fully characterize a market's liquidity, but it gives a clean benchmark for comparison. Using high-frequency Deribit and OKX BTCUSD and ETHUSD inverse options and matching futures over 2020--2026, we describe the level and cross-section of AMU and its evolution through the 2024 regulatory changes (the U.S. spot Bitcoin and Ether ETFs and MiCA). AMU differs only moderately across venues and underlyings; over the option surface it varies mainly through how \emph{often} a wedge appears rather than how large it is; and it compressed markedly around 2024, robustly to the assumed cost level. Panel regressions with cell fixed effects show the compression persists after controlling for spreads, depth, and quote staleness. We read this as descriptive evidence consistent with the entry of participants whose quoting improved market quality, rather than a mere increase in passive depth; the design is not causal.

\textbf{Keywords:} cryptocurrency options, bid--ask spreads, absolute market unfairness, put--call parity, limits to arbitrage, ETF approval, MiCA

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
This paper studies the top-of-book quotes of cryptocurrency option markets, and the premium that market makers require to quote them. The object is not trade prices, but standing quotes: the top of the book is a public good, and its quality is a statement about the market's efficiency. The bid-ask spread is one of the most important frictions that impede the law of one price. TODO: cite something. 

There is no general theory of how wide a spread should be, but it is understood that it is compensation for the market maker to provide liquidity (TODO: cite something). How much compensation is fair is a matter of what model we choose for the behavior of market makers (TODO: cite something). However, there are some special cases where we CAN identify clearly unfair quotes. The best known is the case where identical instruments are quoted on several venues with no friction between transacting on one or another: this the the Nasdaq. In this case the NBBO rule explicitly forbids filling retail market order flow at a price worse than the across-ecn best price. 

There are other situations which might seem similar: The ETF and ADR markets, as well as the On/Off-the-run Treasury markets (TODO cite papers relating to these). But here there are real frictions (liquidity and market access) that legitimately keep the ETF market price vs ETF creation/redemption cost, or ADR and equity price, or on/off-the-run Treasury price, from being equal.

In this paper we show a situation where there are equivalent instruments traded on a single market, where price differentials should not exist at all: These are option markets in european options with matching futures. The put-call parity (PCP) relationship establishes a one-to-one correspondence between a put and a future and a call. The breaking of the PCP has been extensively studied in various markets (TODO: put in citations), and it has been shown that most apparent PCP breaking is due to transaction costs involved in replicating a call from a put. PCP breaking remaining beyond what can be explained by transactions costs are assumed to be idiosyncratic. 

We propose a to use the PCP breaking remaining in quotes after accounting for transaction costs as a measure of the quality of a market or venue for particular option chains. This measure we propose to call AMU (Absolute Market Unfairness), because it can be easily interpreted to represent an incontrovertible symptom of a market with poor liquidity provision. AMU is not meant to characterize a venue's liquidity completely, but it does provide a model-free measure for comparison. 

Our paper will focus on characterizing AMU on cryptocurrency option markets. We describe empirical facts about AMU, including its response to regulatory changes in 2024. We find evidence corroborating the fact that the regulatory changes have introduced new players whose market making behavior improves quoting quality, as opposed to just introducing more liquidity. 


The empirical analysis is organized around three research questions:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{1.} How large is AMU, and does it differ across exchanges and underlyings?\\
\textbf{2.} Did AMU drop significantly upon the 2024 regulatory and institutional regime shift?\\
\textbf{3.} Is any post-2024 drop fully explained by the usual liquidity frictions, or does it partly reflect a lower premium demanded by a new class of parity-enforcing participants?
\end{quote}
We answer all three. Section~\ref{sec:lit} places AMU in the literature. Section~\ref{sec:method} states the cost-plus-premium identity, defines MMA and AMU, and describes the data and panel. Section~\ref{sec:descriptive} presents the level and cross-section of AMU. Section~\ref{sec:regulation} describes the 2024 regulatory shift. Section~\ref{sec:rq2} documents the compression and its robustness to the cost assumption. Section~\ref{sec:regressions} reports the regressions. Section~\ref{sec:concl} concludes.

\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:lit}

\subsection{Put--call parity and crypto derivative efficiency}

\lead{Parity has long been tested, but on trade prices, not quotes.} Testing put--call parity has a long tradition in equity, index, and currency options. Early work shows apparent violations are mostly explained by bid--ask spreads, execution timing, short-sale constraints, taxes, and trading costs \citep{klemkosky1979put, nisbet1992put, kamara1995daily, el1998put, cremers2010deviations}. Crypto options add complications: contracts are often inverse or quanto, collateral and settlement currencies differ from payoff currencies, and the reference index is not always tradable \citep{alexander2023crypto, lucic2024valuation}. This literature tests trade or closing prices. Our object is the standing quotes.

\lead{Recent crypto work studies efficiency; we add a quote-level measure.} Recent work treats derivative efficiency as a market-structure problem. \citet{alexander2024arbitrage} link Bitcoin and Ether options to perpetual futures and find efficiency improving over time, especially at long maturities. \citet{felfoldi2024put} study high-frequency parity in Binance Ether options and show violations shrink once synchronization and costs are handled. We extend this to inverse options and matching futures on Deribit and OKX, and add a comparable basis-point measure of the quoted parity gap.

\subsection{Market quality, bid--ask spreads, and the limits of arbitrage}

\lead{Arbitrage is costly, so a no-arbitrage band opens.} Reading AMU as a symptom of costly, limited arbitrage rests on a simple idea: arbitrage is costly and capital constrained, so a band opens and only wedges that pierce it are worth trading. \citet{amihud1986asset} show spreads affect prices because investors require compensation for illiquidity. \citet{shleifer1997limits} stress that textbook arbitrage is neither riskless nor free in practice. \citet{garleanu2011margin} show how margin and funding constraints create deviations from the law of one price. All of this applies to crypto, where collateral, margin, exchange credit risk, and stablecoin conversion raise the cost of enforcing parity.

\lead{Required mispricing splits into direct cost plus a residual.} The split of required mispricing into direct costs plus a residual is standard. \citet{pontiff1996costly, pontiff2006costly} shows holding costs---including the idiosyncratic risk of an undiversified arbitrageur---dominate, and mispricing survives in proportion to them. \citet{chen2021implied} estimate the no-arbitrage band directly from data. \citet{gromb2010limits} survey the theory: frictions that make convergence risky widen the band. For the market maker, \citet{bollen2004modeling} split the quoted spread into order processing, inventory holding, and adverse selection. AMU is the analogous residual for the parity quote---the part we can call clearly unfair without a model of fair compensation.

\lead{Such bands persist and move with market structure elsewhere.} No-arbitrage bands persist and move with market structure in other markets too. Balance-sheet constraints produced persistent covered interest parity deviations \citep{du2018deviations}; ETF premia persist when the underlying is illiquid or arbitrageurs are constrained \citep{pan2017etf, todorov2021bond}. Crypto runs the other way: regulatory clarity and institutional access plausibly \emph{lowered} the cost of arbitrage capital over our sample, which would compress the band.

\section{The absolute market unfairness: concept and measurement}
\label{sec:method}

The organizing idea is the accounting identity of costly arbitrage:
\begin{equation}
\text{required mispricing} \;=\; \text{direct transaction costs} \;+\; \text{absolute market unfairness}.
\label{eq:amu-identity}
\end{equation}
\lead{AMU is the parity gap left in quotes after direct costs.} A quoted parity deviation is worth acting on only once it covers the direct costs of the round trip. Anything left over is the residual we call AMU. The displaced object is a \emph{quote}: a top-of-book price sitting away from its parity-implied benchmark. We do not decompose that residual. It may reflect a risk premium (counterparty, settlement, convergence, inventory, funding, adverse selection), latency, stale books, tick-size effects, or a low chance of a fill; the limits-to-arbitrage literature catalogues such frictions \citep{pontiff2006costly, chen2021implied, gromb2010limits}, and the spread decomposition names their market-making counterparts \citep{bollen2004modeling}. Our claim is narrower and model-free: whatever its source, a positive AMU is a cost-adjusted parity gap left in the quotes, hence a symptom of imperfect quoting. Everything downstream is net of one direct-cost parameter (\CostPerNotional{} of notional); Section~\ref{sec:rq2} shows the conclusions do not depend on it.

\subsection{Market maker advantage and the absolute market unfairness}
\label{sec:def}

\lead{Parity in crypto options must be written after currency conversion.} The benchmark is still put--call parity, but implementation is contract-specific. Our most liquid contracts---inverse BTCUSD and ETHUSD options on Deribit and OKX---are quoted against a USD index, while collateral, premium, and settlement are in coin. Parity must therefore be written after converting premia and the futures leg to a common currency \citep{alexander2023crypto, lucic2024valuation}: the coin-denominated premia are scaled by the conversion price $S$, and contract size cancels in basis points of notional.

\lead{Four paths implement the arbitrage.} For a matched strike--expiry pair the profit depends on how the arbitrage is run: forward or backward, joining the call or the put side. This gives four paths (Table~\ref{tab:arbitrage_eight_formulas}), each net of a round-trip cost and converted to basis points by $10{,}000/\mathrm{index\_level}$.

\lead{A tickpath is one (pair, path) observation.} Let $m_{i,p,t}$ be the signed basis-point payoff of path $p$ for pair $i$ at time $t$, after conversion and cost adjustment. We call each (pair, path) observation a \emph{tickpath}: every synchronized quote (``tick'') for a matched pair gives four tickpaths, one per path, so a cell $(g,t)$ with $n_{g,t}$ pairs has $4\,n_{g,t}$ tickpaths. Both objects below are averages of $m$ over tickpaths; they differ only in which tickpaths enter.

\textbf{Market maker advantage (MMA)} is the mean of $m_{i,p,t}$ taken over \emph{all} tickpaths in a cell $(g,t)$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MMA}_{g,t}=\frac{1}{4\,n_{g,t}}\sum_{i\in g,t}\sum_{p=1}^{4} m_{i,p,t},
\end{equation}
where $n_{g,t}$ is the number of matched pairs in the cell. The average includes the many paths with no positive profit after costs, so \emph{mean MMA is negative}: it sits near minus the round-trip cost. MMA is the outcome of a maker who joins every quote indiscriminately.

\textbf{The absolute market unfairness (AMU)} comes in two complementary forms, both built from the positive part of $m$. The \emph{conditional} AMU averages $m$ over only the \emph{positive-MMA tickpaths}---those with $m_{i,p,t}>0$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{c}}_{g,t}=\frac{\sum_{i\in g,t}\sum_{p=1}^{4}\max\!\left(0,\,m_{i,p,t}\right)}
{\sum_{i\in g,t}\sum_{p=1}^{4}\mathbf{1}\{m_{i,p,t}>0\}},
\end{equation}
the average \emph{size} of the wedge, given that one exists. Let the \emph{positive-wedge frequency} $\phi_{g,t}$ be the share of the $4\,n_{g,t}$ tickpaths with a positive payoff,
\begin{equation}
\phi_{g,t}=\frac{1}{4\,n_{g,t}}\sum_{i\in g,t}\sum_{p=1}^{4}\mathbf{1}\{m_{i,p,t}>0\},
\end{equation}
and let the \emph{unconditional} AMU average the same positive part over \emph{all} tickpaths:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{u}}_{g,t}=\frac{1}{4\,n_{g,t}}\sum_{i\in g,t}\sum_{p=1}^{4}\max\!\left(0,\,m_{i,p,t}\right)=\phi_{g,t}\,\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{c}}_{g,t}.
\label{eq:amu-decomposition}
\end{equation}
\lead{The two forms factor into frequency times size.} The identity $\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{u}}=\phi\,\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{c}}$ is the paper's organizing decomposition: the per-quote wedge is how \emph{often} a wedge appears ($\phi$) times how \emph{large} it is when it does (conditional AMU). We read the cross-section through this size/frequency split (Figures~\ref{fig:multi_exchange_amu_by_strike}--\ref{fig:multi_exchange_amu_by_tte}); the time series and regressions use the unconditional AMU. Both are in basis points of notional.

\lead{AMU is the expected profit of an imaginary maker who joins only the favorable side.} Read AMU through an imaginary maker who joins a quote only when the parity payoff is in his favor. It is an expectation, not a realized profit: whether he is filled depends on queue position and on a taker crossing. The bid--ask spread already pays him for the usual costs of making a market---order processing, inventory, adverse selection, queue risk. AMU is separate: it measures only how differently the two equivalent instruments---the real quote and its parity-implied synthetic---are priced against one another. MMA, conditional AMU, and unconditional AMU are three summaries of the same tickpath distribution of $m$: its mean over all tickpaths, its mean over the positive ones, and the mean of its positive part.

\lead{The cost assumption only sets the scale.} Trading costs are knowable but venue-dependent and messy. We assume a fixed proportional cost of \CostPerNotional{} per round trip. Two things make this harmless. First, the pre/post dynamics of unconditional AMU barely move with the cost level, which we check over a cost grid (Figure~\ref{fig:amu_by_cost_pre_post}); the conditional form is more cost-sensitive, one reason the headline results use the unconditional measure. Second, a fixed cost across venues keeps cross-exchange comparisons coherent. The cost only sets AMU's scale.

\subsection{The range of arbitrage configurations}

\lead{Geometrically, AMU compares a real quote to its synthetic twin.} It is useful to see what AMU is measuring geometrically. For a given option leg---say the call---the quotes define a \emph{real} market $[C_{bid},C_{ask}]$, while parity combined with the put and the matching futures defines a \emph{synthetic} market $[C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid},C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}]$, where $C^{\mathrm{syn}}=P+(F-K)e^{-rT}$. Both intervals live on the same price axis, and their relative position determines whether, and how strongly, an arbitrage exists. Figure~\ref{fig:arb_paths_crossed_noncrossed} shows the three qualitatively distinct configurations.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\maybeincludegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{artifacts/arbitrage_paths_crossed_noncrossed.pdf}
\caption{Real quoted versus parity-implied (synthetic) option market. When the markets are crossed and disjoint ($C_{ask}<C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid}$, or the symmetric case), there is a single guaranteed riskless arbitrage. When one market is nested inside the other, or when the two straddle each other, there is no guaranteed profit but two paths carry a \emph{potential} market-maker advantage, realized only if the maker's quote is joined and then hit. In the nested case these are the forward and backward join-call paths ($C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid}-C_{bid}$ and $C_{ask}-C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}$), with the join-put payoffs equal to their negatives (exactly so when the futures market has zero spread; the trading cost need not be zero); in the straddling case they are the forward join-call and forward join-put paths ($C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid}-C_{bid}$ and $C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}-C_{ask}$). The latter two configurations are what AMU aggregates. Schematic, illustrative levels.}
\label{fig:arb_paths_crossed_noncrossed}
\end{figure}

\lead{Only the crossed case is a guaranteed arbitrage.} In the crossed case the real ask lies below the synthetic bid (or vice versa); buying one and selling the other locks in the gap. In the nested and straddling cases there is no guaranteed profit: the payoff depends on which side is joined and whether a taker crosses. These conditional states are what MMA and AMU aggregate over the surface.

\lead{AMU is not the spread; it is where the quote sits.} MMA and AMU do not measure the quoted half-spread itself---the compensation makers demand for liquidity provision. They measure where the quotes \emph{sit}: each real quote must track its synthetic counterpart within the cost cushion (Section~\ref{sec:noamu-band}), and AMU is what stands outside that tolerance. This is the through-price gap of the NBBO analogy from the introduction, with the parity-implied synthetic market as the second venue.

\subsection{The no-AMU band and the shape of the MMA distribution}
\label{sec:noamu-band}

\lead{How far can a quote move before it is unfair?} The configurations in Figure~\ref{fig:arb_paths_crossed_noncrossed} raise a sharper question: for a \emph{given} synthetic market, how far can a real quote move before it becomes unfair? The answer pins each real quote to its synthetic counterpart within a tolerance set by the trading cost, and it fixes the shape that the MMA distribution ``should'' take.

\lead{Two paths bound the fair call ask.} Consider the real call ask $C_{ask}$. Two of the four paths in Table~\ref{tab:arbitrage_eight_formulas} contain it---forward/join-put and backward/join-call. Requiring both payoffs to be non-positive (no AMU) yields
\begin{equation}
P_{ask}+(F_{ask}-K)e^{-rT}-\mathrm{cost}
\;\le\; C_{ask} \;\le\;
P_{ask}+(F_{bid}-K)e^{-rT}+\mathrm{cost}.
\label{eq:cask-band}
\end{equation}
\lead{A fair quote must track its synthetic within a cost cushion.} Both bounds use the \emph{same-side} put quote $P_{ask}$, so the band is centred on the synthetic ask evaluated at the futures mid, $C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}\equiv P_{ask}+(F_{mid}-K)e^{-rT}$, with half-width $\mathrm{cost}-\tfrac12(F_{ask}-F_{bid})e^{-rT}$. The symmetric calculation pins $C_{bid}$ to $C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid}$. In words: a fair real market must track the synthetic market quote by quote, to within $\pm\mathrm{cost}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:amu_fair_band_synthetic}).

\lead{The band's width comes from the cost.} The role of the cost is easy to mis-state. It is \emph{not} that $C_{ask}$ merely lies inside the synthetic bid--ask; that statement would carry no cost at all. Rather, $C_{ask}$ is tied to the synthetic \emph{ask} within a $\pm\mathrm{cost}$ cushion, which is what gives the band its width. With $\mathrm{cost}=0$ and a positive futures spread, the interval in \eqref{eq:cask-band} inverts and becomes empty: no strictly fair ask exists. As frictions vanish, the band collapses and the real market is forced onto the synthetic one---the law of one price.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\maybeincludegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{artifacts/amu_fair_band_synthetic.pdf}
\caption{No AMU requires each real quote to track its matching synthetic quote within $\pm\mathrm{cost}$. The band centres are the frictionless (no-cost) synthetic prices $P_{bid}+(F_{bid}-K)e^{-rT}$ and $P_{ask}+(F_{ask}-K)e^{-rT}$; the trading cost buys the $\pm\mathrm{cost}$ cushion around each. As costs shrink, the real market is squeezed onto the synthetic market. Schematic, illustrative levels.}
\label{fig:amu_fair_band_synthetic}
\end{figure}

\lead{Uniform quoting would imply a flat MMA rectangle.} Equation~\eqref{eq:cask-band} also fixes the shape of the MMA distribution. Write $u=C_{ask}-C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}$; up to the futures half-spread the two conjugate $C_{ask}$ payoffs are $u-\mathrm{cost}$ (forward, join put) and $-\mathrm{cost}-u$ (backward, join call). As $C_{ask}$ ranges over its fair band $u\in(-\mathrm{cost},\mathrm{cost})$, each sweeps linearly over $(-2\,\mathrm{cost},0)$. If makers placed quotes with no preferred point inside the band, the implied MMA would be \emph{uniform} on $(-2\,\mathrm{cost},0)$: a rectangle of width $2\,\mathrm{cost}$ immediately to the left of zero. That rectangle is the no-information benchmark (dashed in Figure~\ref{fig:amu_schematic_mma_distribution}).

\lead{Competition instead concentrates MMA near minus the cost.} The observed distribution instead peaks sharply near $-\mathrm{cost}$. Two things point there, and it is worth separating them. Pooling the conjugate paths---which share the call-ask and put-ask quotes and whose payoffs sum to $-2\,\mathrm{cost}$---makes the distribution symmetric about $-\mathrm{cost}$ by construction. That it is also sharply \emph{peaked} there, rather than spread uniformly across the fair band, is the economic content: a maker who improves one side's MMA lowers the conjugate by the same amount, so the symmetric quote is the one that concedes no advantage. Competitive quoting thus keeps the modal MMA near $-\mathrm{cost}$ (Figures~\ref{fig:amu_schematic_mma_distribution} and~\ref{fig:all_markets_avg_4_spreads}). Stale or aggressive quotes populate the tails; the right tail crossing zero is the AMU.

\subsection{Data and sample}

The sample uses high-frequency Deribit and OKX BTCUSD and ETHUSD inverse options and matching-expiry futures from Tardis.dev \citep{tardis_main}, spanning 2020 through June 2026, with synchronized best bids and asks for calls, puts, and futures. Ticks are processed with our open-source codebases---\texttt{balazs14/TardisProject} and \texttt{balazs14/publications} (subfolder \texttt{AMU-liquidity-premium})---which implement synchronization, filtering, parity-path construction, and AMU aggregation \citep{kralik_tardisproject_repo, kralik_amu_publication_repo}. The same calculation applies across the full horizon, so pre/post-2024 comparisons are coherent.

Table~\ref{tab:summary_daily_option_coverage} summarizes coverage. Deribit lists a wider surface---more contracts, expirations, and strikes per day---while spreads are broadly comparable, slightly wider for ETH on OKX. \section{The level and cross-section of the absolute market unfairness}
\label{sec:descriptive}

\subsection{The shape of the MMA distribution and why it peaks below zero}
\label{sec:peak}

\lead{The MMA density peaks at minus the cost.} The most informative object in the data is the shape of the MMA distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:all_markets_avg_4_spreads}). Pooled across markets and paths, the density peaks sharply at minus the trading cost---near $-\CostBp$ basis points---and falls away on both sides. Zero splits the region where the maker cannot profit from the positive tail that feeds AMU.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\maybeincludegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{artifacts/all_markets_avg_4_spreads.pdf}
\caption{Average density of the four MMA paths across all markets. The distribution peaks at minus the round-trip cost (dashed line); everything to the left of zero yields no maker profit, and only the positive tail to the right of zero contributes to AMU.}
\label{fig:all_markets_avg_4_spreads}
\end{figure}


\lead{The peak is where competition drove the wedge down to cost.} The reading is central to the paper. Makers improve quotes only as much as competition forces, so the mass sits at $-\text{cost}$: in the modal state the wedge is competed down to about the cost of the trade. Quoting is noisy, so the distribution is not a point mass. Quotes too good for the buy side fill the left tail; quotes too bad leave a positive wedge and fill the right tail. The right tail's conditional mean is the conditional AMU, its share is $\phi$, and their product is the unconditional AMU. AMU is nonzero because repricing is not instant: after a shock some quotes are briefly stale or mis-set. Faster, denser competition erases both tails. Everything that follows measures this right tail across venues, the surface, and time.

\subsection{RQ1: AMU across exchanges, moneyness, and maturity}
\label{sec:rq1}

\lead{Venues differ moderately; the thinnest book has the most AMU.} The global averages (Table~\ref{tab:amu_summary}) show venues differ moderately, not on a simple BTC/ETH split. The standout is ETH on OKX, the thinnest book (Table~\ref{tab:summary_daily_option_coverage}), as expected if AMU tracks quoting quality: the least deep book leaves the largest wedge above cost. Positive MMA is not rare: a large fraction of ticks carry at least one positive-MMA path, so the positive-part average is a routine phenomenon, not isolated glitches. The ratio of the unconditional to the conditional column recovers $\phi$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:amu-decomposition}).

\lead{Over the surface, AMU varies mainly through frequency, not size.} The surface shape is sharper than the venue averages and regular across all four markets. In Figure~\ref{fig:multi_exchange_amu_by_strike}, the positive MMA quote frequency $\phi$ (lower panel) traces a deep V---near one in the wings, a few percent at the money---while the conditional size (upper panel) is flat. AMU is smallest for month-scale, near-the-money contracts and largest in the illiquid corners. Over the surface it is the \emph{incidence} of mis-quoting that varies, not its size.

AMU differs across venues, but position on the surface---distance from the money and from a one-month horizon---explains far more of its variation than venue does. Both patterns line up with liquidity: the wedge is largest where books are thin and quotes stale.

\section{The 2024 regulatory shift}
\label{sec:regulation}

\lead{Regulation, not parity, changed in 2024.} Three milestones cluster in 2024. On January 10 the SEC approved spot Bitcoin ETPs, connecting crypto price risk to regulated custody, authorized-participant arbitrage, and mainstream brokerage \citep{sec_spot_bitcoin_etp_2024}. In May it approved spot Ether ETFs, which began trading in July \citep{sec_spot_ether_etp_2024}. In parallel, MiCA came into force in stages: stablecoin provisions in mid-2024, the CASP licensing regime at end-2024 \citep{esma_mica_2024}. None of this changed put--call parity; it changed who can hold, finance, and hedge crypto exposure at scale, and under what legal certainty.

\lead{Bigger, regulated players plausibly need a smaller premium.} This is where AMU is useful as a diagnostic. Whatever keeps a parity wedge open, it is plausibly cheaper to bear for a large, diversified, well-capitalized institution than for the crypto-native firms that dominated before: regulation eases custody and legal risk, balance-sheet depth eases funding risk, diversification eases inventory and convergence risk. If 2024 brought such participants into quoting these markets---directly, or by financing those who do---one would expect the wedge to shrink, beyond any gain in the observable frictions. This is a working hypothesis, not something we identify cleanly: we treat 2024 as a descriptive regime marker and ask whether the evidence fits.

\lead{This gives two descriptive questions.} Two descriptive questions follow: did AMU drop around the 2024 changes, and does the drop line up with the observable frictions (spreads, depth, staleness), or persist beyond them? Sections~\ref{sec:rq2} and~\ref{sec:regressions} take these in turn.

\section{Regression analysis}
\label{sec:regressions}

\subsection{Empirical design}
\label{sec:design}

\lead{The outcome is cell-level unconditional AMU.} The dependent variable in all four regressions is the cell-level unconditional AMU, $\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{u}}_{g,t}=\phi_{g,t}\,\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{c}}_{g,t}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:amu-decomposition})---a quote-level, panel-observable object. Regressors enter in three blocks: the post-2024 indicator $\mathrm{Post}_t$; segment dummies (exchange, underlying, moneyness, maturity); and three liquidity frictions,
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Spr}_{g,t}&=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\texttt{mean\_call\_spread\_bp}_{g,t}+\texttt{mean\_put\_spread\_bp}_{g,t}\right),\\
\mathrm{Depth}_{g,t}&=\log\!\left(\texttt{mean\_min\_quote\_size\_dollar}_{g,t}\right),\\
\mathrm{Stale}_{g,t}&=\text{mean stale-quote fraction across call, put, and spot legs}.
\end{align}
\lead{Frictions enter as descriptors, not to identify a premium.} The direct-cost term is already subtracted inside every path payoff (Table~\ref{tab:arbitrage_eight_formulas}), so AMU is the wedge that survives above that floor (Eq.~\ref{eq:amu-identity}). We add the friction variables not to identify a structural premium but to describe: how much of AMU, and of its 2024 change, moves with observable book quality---wider spreads, thinner depth, staler quotes \citep{amihud1986asset, shleifer1997limits, garleanu2011margin, bollen2004modeling}---and how much does not.

\lead{The break date is January 10, 2024.} $\mathrm{Post}_t$ equals one on and after January 10, 2024---the Bitcoin ETP approval, first of the 2024 milestones---treated as a single regime date rather than separate shocks.

\lead{We run four nested specifications.} We estimate four specifications, adding regressors in blocks:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{u}}_{g,t}&=\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}\mathrm{Post}_t+\varepsilon_{g,t},\\
\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{u}}_{g,t}&=\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}\mathrm{Post}_t+\gamma_g+\varepsilon_{g,t},\\
\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{u}}_{g,t}&=\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}\mathrm{Post}_t+\beta_{\mathrm{OKX}}\mathrm{OKX}_g+\beta_{\mathrm{ETH}}\mathrm{ETH}_g+\beta_{\mathrm{ATM}}\mathrm{ATM}_g+\beta_{\mathrm{NE}}\mathrm{NearExp}_g+\varepsilon_{g,t},\\
\mathrm{AMU}^{\mathrm{u}}_{g,t}&=\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}\mathrm{Post}_t+\beta_{\mathrm{Depth}}\mathrm{Depth}_{g,t}+\beta_{\mathrm{Spr}}\mathrm{Spr}_{g,t}+\beta_{\mathrm{Stale}}\mathrm{Stale}_{g,t}+\gamma_g+\varepsilon_{g,t},
\end{align}
all with a constant and HC1 standard errors. Specification (1) is the raw regime effect. Specification (2) adds cell fixed effects $\gamma_g$, isolating the within-cell shift. Specification (3) instead adds the segment dummies as pooled OLS: there the dummies are the objects of interest (RQ1), so we estimate rather than absorb them. Specification (4) adds the frictions with cell fixed effects. We prefer cell fixed effects because the surface dependence is strongly nonlinear (Figures~\ref{fig:multi_exchange_amu_by_strike}--\ref{fig:multi_exchange_amu_by_tte}); linear dummies would leave segment structure in the residual. In (4), $\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}$ is the within-cell change net of both the frictions and the full segment structure. Table~\ref{tab:regressor_abbreviations} defines the abbreviations.

\subsection{Results}

\lead{We use the regressions to check that AMU behaves like a market-quality statistic.} We do not try to identify why AMU fell. We ask whether it behaves as a good market-quality measure should. Two things are required: it should fall when the market is independently believed to have improved (the 2024 institutionalization), and that fall should not be an artifact of which contracts trade. Table~\ref{tab:regression_coefficients} checks both. The columns are (1) the raw post-2024 shift; (2) plus cell fixed effects; (3) segment dummies (pooled OLS); (4) the frictions with cell fixed effects. Standard errors are HC1; the \emph{Eff} columns report the standardized effect $\beta\,\mathrm{std}(X)/\mathrm{std}(Y)$.

\lead{AMU is markedly lower after 2024.} \emph{Specification (1).} The post-2024 coefficient is negative and large. AMU is much lower after 2024---the period the market is widely agreed to have improved. This is the first thing a market-quality measure should show, and answers RQ2.

\lead{The drop is within cells, not a change in contract mix.} \emph{Specification (2).} Adding cell fixed effects barely moves the post-2024 coefficient. The drop happens \emph{within} fixed strike--maturity--venue cells over time, not because the traded mix shifted. This rules out the main composition artifact---the second thing we need.

\lead{AMU differs across segments, but only slightly.} \emph{Specification (3).} With segment dummies (pooled OLS), AMU differs by exchange, underlying, moneyness, and maturity---all significant but small. Venue and underlying move AMU far less than position on the surface. The low $R^2$ is expected: linear dummies cannot capture the nonlinear surface, which is why (4) uses fixed effects. This answers RQ1.

\lead{Most of the drop survives the friction controls.} \emph{Specification (4).} Adding spreads, depth, and staleness (staleness strongest) shrinks the post-2024 coefficient but does not remove it: a large part of the drop is not accounted for by these observable book-quality measures. The positive tail is, as expected, made of transiently mispriced, slow-to-update quotes. We read the surviving drop as consistent with quoting that improved beyond deeper passive books---but the design is descriptive and identifies neither a mechanism nor a cause. This is our reading of RQ3.

\lead{The regressions corroborate AMU as a market-quality measure.} Taken together: AMU differs significantly but modestly across segments (RQ1); it is much lower after 2024 (RQ2); and most of that drop is within cells and survives the friction controls (RQ3). Because 2024 is independently known as a period of market improvement, AMU moving the way a market-quality statistic should---down, within cells, beyond the obvious frictions---is what gives us confidence in it as a measure. We do not claim to identify which participants drove the change.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

\lead{AMU is a model-free symptom of imperfect quoting.} We propose \emph{Absolute Market Unfairness}: the cost-adjusted put--call-parity violation left in the standing quotes of inverse cryptocurrency option markets. It is model-free and comparable in basis points---an incontrovertible sign that the top of the book is not where frictionless parity would put it---not a full account of a venue's liquidity or a cleanly identified risk premium. It says nothing about fundamental mispricing and guarantees no dollar profit.

\lead{AMU is small across venues, shaped by the surface, and fell in 2024.} Using Deribit and OKX BTCUSD and ETHUSD options from 2020 through June 2026: AMU differs across venues and underlyings, but little next to its variation over the surface, where it is governed mainly by how often a wedge appears---smallest for month-scale, near-the-money contracts, largest in the illiquid corners. It fell sharply around 2024, tracking the ETF and MiCA dates, robustly to the cost level, and most of the drop survives controls for spreads, depth, and staleness within cells. This is consistent with the entry of participants whose quoting improved market quality, not just more passive depth---though the design is descriptive and does not identify the mechanism. The value of AMU is that it turns a qualitative worry about quote quality into a model-free number, comparable across venues, underlyings, and maturities, and trackable through regulatory change.
