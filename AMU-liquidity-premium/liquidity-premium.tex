\title{\textbf{Market Unfairness and the Compression of Bid--Ask Spreads in Cryptocurrency Option Markets}}

\author{Bal\'azs Kr\'alik\thanks{Corresponding Author. Assistant Professor at Corvinus University of Budapest, Institute of Finance, F\H{o}v\'am square 8. Budapest, Hungary, 1093. E-mail: balazs.kralik@uni-corvinus.hu.}, N\'ora Felf\"oldi-Sz\H{u}cs\thanks{Associate Professor at Corvinus University of Budapest, Institute of Finance.}, Tam\'as Rutai\thanks{Student at Corvinus University of Budapest.}, Kata V\'aradi\thanks{Associate Professor at Corvinus University of Budapest, Institute of Finance.}}
\date{}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Put--call parity ties a European call, a put, and a matching future into a one-to-one correspondence, so on a single venue their standing quotes should be mutually consistent up to transaction costs. We propose to call the inconsistency in top of book quotes \emph{market unfairness} (MU): the part of the quoted put--call-parity violation that remains, at the top of the book, after netting out direct transaction costs. Unlike the put--call-parity literature, which tests \emph{trade} prices, MU is a statement about \emph{quotes}. It is a model-free symptom of poor liquidity provision---in the spirit of the best-execution (NBBO) rules that police quote quality where identical instruments trade frictionlessly on several venues. It does not fully characterize a market's liquidity, but it gives a clean benchmark for comparison. Using high-frequency Deribit and OKX BTCUSD and ETHUSD inverse options and matching futures over 2020--2026, we describe the level and cross-section of market unfairness and its evolution through the 2024 regulatory changes (the U.S. spot Bitcoin and Ether ETFs and MiCA). Market unfairness differs only moderately across venues and underlyings, but varies significantly over the option surface and it compressed markedly around 2024. We find evidence consistent with the entry of participants whose quoting improved market quality, as opposed to a mere increase in passive depth or tightening of spreads.

\textbf{Keywords:} cryptocurrency options, bid--ask spreads, market unfairness, put--call parity, limits to arbitrage, ETF approval, MiCA

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
This paper studies the top-of-book quotes of cryptocurrency option markets, and the premium that market makers require to quote them. The object is not transaction prices, but standing quotes: the quality of the top of as a measure the market's efficiency. For example, the smaller the bid-ask spread, the better the quality of the market, the closer the market conforms to the law of one price \citep{demsetz1968cost, amihud1986asset}.

There is no general theory of how wide a spread \textit{should} be, what \textit{would} be fair, but it is understood that it is compensation for the market maker to provide liquidity \citep{demsetz1968cost, ho1981optimal, glosten1985bid}. How much compensation is fair is a matter of what model we choose for the behavior of market makers \citep{ho1981optimal, bollen2004modeling}. However, there are some special cases where we \emph{can} identify clearly unfair quotes. The best known is the case where identical instruments are quoted on several venues with no friction between transacting on one or another: this is the Nasdaq. There the best-execution rules under Regulation NMS \citep{sec_reg_nms_2005} forbid filling retail market order flow at a price worse than the National Best Bid and Offer across venues.

There are other situations which might seem similar: The ETF and ADR markets, as well as the On/Off-the-run Treasury markets \citep{pan2017etf, gagnon2010multimarket, krishnamurthy2002bond}. But here there are real frictions (liquidity and market access) that legitimately keep the ETF market price vs ETF creation/redemption cost, or ADR and equity price, or on/off-the-run Treasury price, from being equal.

In this paper we show a situation where there are equivalent instruments traded on a single market, where price differentials should not exist at all: These are markets in European options with matching futures. The put-call parity (PCP) relationship establishes a one-to-one correspondence between a put, a futures, and a call. The breaking of the PCP has been extensively studied in various markets \citep{klemkosky1979put, kamara1995daily, cremers2010deviations, ofek2004limited}, and it has been shown that most apparent PCP breaking is due to transaction costs involved in replicating a call from a put. PCP breaking remaining beyond what can be explained by transactions costs are assumed to be idiosyncratic. 

We propose to use the PCP breaking remaining in quotes after accounting for transaction costs for a new purpose: a measure of the quality of a market or venue for particular option chains. This measure we propose to call market unfairness (MU), where ``unfairness'' is a standardized statistic rather than a welfare judgment: it flags quotes that sit outside the cost-implied no-arbitrage band, a symptom of imperfect quote consistency. Market unfairness is not meant to characterize a venue's market quality completely, but it does provide a model-free measure for comparison as long a trading costs are known. 

Our paper will focus on characterizing MU on cryptocurrency option markets. We describe empirical facts about market unfairness, including its response to regulatory changes in 2024. We find evidence corroborating the fact that the regulatory changes have introduced new players whose market making behavior improves quoting quality, not just increased volumes or tightened spreads.

\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:lit}

\subsection{The limits of put-call-parity arbitrage in general}

First, there is plenty of literature linking PCP breaking to bid-ask spreads.  \citet{amihud1986asset} show spreads affect prices because investors require compensation for illiquidity. \citet{shleifer1997limits} stress that textbook arbitrage is neither riskless nor free in practice. Testing put--call parity has a long tradition in equity, index, and currency options. Early work shows apparent violations are mostly explained by bid--ask spreads, execution timing, short-sale constraints, taxes, and trading costs \citep{klemkosky1979put, nisbet1992put, kamara1995daily, el1998put, cremers2010deviations}.

PCP arbitrage involves opening a costless portfolio of put, call and underlying (or futures) contracts which has strictly positive payout. This can be phrased as a real and synthetic price for an option, which are supposedly equal. The split of the real--synthetic price difference into direct costs plus a residual is customary, and the study of this residual is the object of the PCP breaking literature. Market unfairness is the analogous residual for the parity quote, the object of this paper. \citet{pontiff1996costly, pontiff2006costly} shows holding costs---including the idiosyncratic risk of an undiversified arbitrageur---dominate, but mispricing survives. \citet{chen2021implied} estimate the no-arbitrage band directly from data. \citet{gromb2010limits} survey the theory: frictions that make convergence risky widen the band. For the market maker, \citet{bollen2004modeling} split the quoted spread into order processing, inventory holding, and adverse selection. 

\subsection{PCP in crypto derivative markets}

 Crypto options add complications: contracts are often inverse or quanto, collateral and settlement currencies differ from payoff currencies, and the reference index is not always tradable \citep{alexander2023crypto, lucic2024valuation}. \citet{garleanu2011margin} show how margin and funding constraints create deviations from the law of one price. \citet{alexander2024arbitrage} link Bitcoin and Ether options to perpetual futures and find efficiency improving over time, especially at long maturities. \citet{felfoldi2024put} study high-frequency parity in Binance Ether options and show violations shrink once synchronization and costs are handled. We extend this to inverse options and matching futures on Deribit and OKX.

Before proposing a new measure, we describe the crypto option market through the classical liquidity metrics. For each exchange and underlying we track several measures over 2020--2026, aggregating over strike and maturity to a daily series and smoothing with a one-month rolling mean (Figures~\ref{fig:friction_timeseries_direct} and~\ref{fig:friction_timeseries_dynamic}). 

Some are direct properties of the standing quotes: 
\begin{itemize}
  \item the average put--call bid--ask \emph{spread} in units of equivalent dollar amount of the price difference $\mathrm{Spr}^{\mathrm{\$}}$
  \item the quote \emph{depth} $\mathrm{Depth}^{\mathrm{\$}}$, the dollar-equivalent size resting at the top of book. Note that this is sensitive to the contract size convention, which we took into account. 
  \item the \emph{staleness} $\mathrm{Stale}$, the fraction of quotes that fail to refresh within the sampling interval. Note that this staleness does not necessarily mean a market making failure, this can occur when the market simply does not move.  
\end{itemize}

We also consider the following dynamic illiquidity and resiliency measures, computed for each strike--maturity cell from its intraday series and averaged over the strike--maturity surface:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{Amihud} illiquidity ratio \citep{amihud2002illiquidity}, the average of $|\text{return}|/\text{volume}$ over the cell's five-minute call and put series---how far the price moves per unit traded---with volume measured in option-\emph{premium} dollars (contracts times contract size times the sum of the call and put mid prices);
  \item the \emph{spread recovery time}, a resiliency measure---the third dimension of liquidity alongside tightness and depth \citep{kyle1985continuous, foucault2013market}. After a \emph{large} widening---the spread's deviation from its centered rolling one-hour baseline crossing above two robust standard deviations (from the day's median absolute deviation)---we fit an AR(1) decay to the deviation over the following hour and report its half-life $-\ln 2/\ln\rho$ in minutes, the time for a shock to die out by half; we compute it for both the bp and the dollar spread. Conditioning on genuine widenings, in the spirit of the order-book resiliency literature \citep{degryse2005resiliency, large2007measuring}, discards the small idiosyncratic quote wiggles that otherwise leave an unconditional AR(1) near unit-root and uninformative.
\end{itemize}

An interesting opportunity to observe the market's liquidity evolution is the 2024 regulatory and institutional regime shift (Section~\ref{sec:regulation}). 
Figure~\ref{fig:friction_timeseries_direct} staleness falling markedly around 2024. Dollar depth rises continuously, showing a general deepening of the market over time, whereas the dollar spread shows no trend. The Amihud illiquidity ratio and the spread-recovery times (Figure~\ref{fig:friction_timeseries_dynamic}) also show regime change. Traded volume (Figure~\ref{fig:premium_volume_timeseries}) is also on a seemingly unstoppable exponential growth path, which does not obviously display a regime change in 2024. 

Each channel captures a facet of liquidity, each of them are on a flat or improving trend, but can we say that an investor is better off now than before? Large players can be happy as the Amihud and recovery statistics (which characterize market impact related loss) have definitely improved, but retail investors, who in general will care about market impact, will on average they face non-improving spreads.

The rest of this paper develops MU, a complementary, model-free measure to test market fairness that is orthogonal to the above market liquidity measures. It is built only from standing quote information and applies PCP consitency conditions to filter out unfair markets. We'll also show that this measure decreased in 2024, showing that the regulatory changes brought benefits to the retail investor too.

\section{Market Unfairness: concept and measurement}
\label{sec:method}

% The organizing idea is the accounting identity of costly arbitrage:
% \begin{equation}
% \text{real--synthetic quote difference} \;\leftarrow\; \text{synthetic quote transaction costs} \;+\; \text{absolute market unfairness}.
% \label{eq:amu-identity}
% \end{equation}

Market makers post passive orders to the book. If and when the passive order is hit, the resulting exposure can be fully hedged via a PCP arbitrage loop. For example if our bid for a call is lifted, we are long the call, which we can hedge by going short futures and a corresponding put. If we got the call too cheap, we can lock in risk-free profit. A market maker seeking risk-free profits might wait for such parity breaking to occur, and join or improve a book, waiting for takers. The expected payoff is strictly positive, even though issues of queue position and the disappearance of the arbitrage path exist. We deal with these microstructure issues by studying intraday snapshot data, where the quotes involved in the arbitrage are all present at the same time. In addition, the microstructure issues only limit the dollar value and not the sign of this opportunity.

We quantify this opportunity for market makers as the \emph{market maker advantage} (MMA) which is the payout of being filled and fully hedged via PCP. This is negative most of the time. But there are times where the market maker can expect a profit if filled:  we call the extent to which such opportunities exist \emph{market unfairness}, MU. The occurrence of cost-adjusted parity deviations at the top of the book can reasonably be called unfair, since a market maker could improve the PCP violating quote with a strictly positive expected payoff. 

% A parity deviation in the quote invites the market maker to join the unfair quote, but only if getting filled can produce riskless profit. This potential riskless profit we call market unfairness. We do not decompose the direct cost. Primarily it reflects direct trading costs imposed by the exchange, but also a risk premium (counterparty, settlement, convergence, inventory, funding, adverse selection), latency, stale books, tick-size effects, or a low chance of a fill; the limits-to-arbitrage literature catalogues such frictions \citep{pontiff2006costly, chen2021implied, gromb2010limits}, and the spread decomposition names their market-making counterparts \citep{bollen2004modeling}. Our claim is narrower and model-free: whatever its source, a positive $U_{u}$ is a cost-adjusted parity gap left in the quotes, hence a symptom of imperfect quoting. Everything downstream is net of one direct-cost parameter (\CostPerNotional{} of notional); Section~\ref{sec:rq2} shows the conclusions empirically do not strongly depend on the exact choice of the direct-cost parameter.

\subsection{Market maker advantage and market unfairness precisely defined}
\label{sec:def}

A source of complexity in executing our study is the fact that the most liquid contracts are inverse. We use  BTCUSD and ETHUSD options on Deribit and OKX, which are quoted against a USD index, but collateral, premium, and settlement are in coin. Parity must therefore be written after converting premia and the futures leg to a common currency \citep{alexander2023crypto, lucic2024valuation}: the coin-denominated premia are then scaled by the conversion price $S$ as of the time of posting the quote. 

For a matched strike--expiry put-call pair the profit depends on how the arbitrage is run: backward or forward, joining the call or the put side. By convention \emph{forward} means arbitrage involving going \emph{long} futures and vice versa. The index $p$ for \emph{path} can take 4 values. In every path the maker \emph{joins} a standing quote rather than crossing it: he posts and profits only if a taker hits the standing quote. For instance, in the backward/join-call-bid path she buys the call at $C_{bid}$; in the forward/join-put-bid path she sells it at $C_{bid}$.

The profit formulas for the four paths (Table~\ref{tab:arbitrage_eight_formulas}), are net of the round-trip cost of executing one \emph{trade unit}. A trade unit is the number of contracts having notional equal to the index value. So for example on OKX, 100 contracts of BTCUSD options would correspond to a trade unit, given that the contract multiplier is 0.01 on this market.

Let $m_{i,p,t}$ be the signed dollar one-trade-unit payoff of path $p$ for \emph{pair} $i$ at time $t$, after conversion and cost adjustment. We call each (pair, path) observation a \emph{tickpath}: every synchronized quote (\emph{tick}) for a matched pair gives four tickpaths.

A \emph{tick} is indexed by (pair, time). A \emph{pair} is indexed by (exchange, underlying, strike, expiry).

We aggregate tickpaths in \emph{cells} $(g,t)$ with cell $g$ corresponding to a group of pairs with exchange, underlying, time, strike, expiration, each in some range. A cell with $n_{g,t}$ pairs has $4\,n_{g,t}$ tickpaths. 

\textbf{Market maker advantage (MMA)} is the mean of $m_{i,p,t}$ taken over \emph{all} tickpaths in a cell $(g,t)$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MMA}_{g,t}=\frac{1}{4n_{g,t}}\sum_{i\in g}\sum_{p=1}^{4} m_{i,p,t},
\end{equation}
where $n_{g,t}$ is the number of pairs in cell $g,t$. The average includes the many paths with non-positive profit after costs, as well as the positive ones. 

We can also compute MMA for $t$, summing out $g$, or vice versa. We can also form MMA without the index: a global average over all tickpaths. Just to be precise: MMA is an average over tickpaths, and not over cells. To clear up potential misunderstandings, some integrated formulas for different aggregation levels are explicitly:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathrm{MMA}=\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i,p,t} m_{i,p,t}, \quad
\mathrm{MMA}_{g}=\frac{1}{4n_{g}}\sum_{i\in g,p,t} m_{i,p,t}, \\
\mathrm{MMA}_{d}=\frac{1}{4n_d}\sum_{t\in d,i,p} m_{i,p,t}, \quad
\mathrm{MMA}_{g,d}=\frac{1}{4n_{g,d}}\sum_{t\in d,i\in g,p} m_{i,p,t},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $n$, $n_g$, $n_d$, are $n_{g,d}$ are the number of ticks, ticks in a cell, ticks in a period $d$ (e.g. a day or a number of days), and ticks in a cell-period, respectively. 

\textbf{Market unfairness (MU)} comes in two complementary forms, both built from the positive part of $m$. The \emph{conditional unfairness} $U_{c}$ averages $m$ over only the \emph{positive-MMA tickpaths}---those with $m_{i,p,t}>0$:
\begin{equation}
U_{c\,g,d}=\frac{\sum_{i\in g,p,t\in d}\max\!\left(0,\,m_{i,p,t}\right)}
{\sum_{i\in g,p,t\in d,}\mathbf{1}\{m_{i,p,t}>0\}},
\end{equation}
the average \emph{size} of the arbitrage, given that one exists. Let the \emph{unfairness rate} $R_{g,d}$ be the share of the $4\,n_{g,d}$ tickpaths with a positive payoff,
\begin{equation}
R_{g,d}=\frac{1}{4\,n_{g,d}}\sum_{i\in g,p,t\in d}\mathbf{1}\{m_{i,p,t}>0\},
\end{equation}
and let the \emph{unconditional unfairness} $U_{u}$ average the same positive part over \emph{all} tickpaths:
\begin{equation}
U_{u\,g,d}=\frac{1}{4\,n_{g,d}}\sum_{i\in g,p,t\in d}\max\!\left(0,\,m_{i,p,t}\right)=R_{g,d}\,U_{c\,g,d}.
\label{eq:amu-decomposition}
\end{equation}

As is often the case with measuring long-tail averages, $U_c$ and $U_u$ are sensitive to outliers and therefore sensitive to capping or censoring $m$. Being dollar denominated, they also suffer from scaling issues. As we'll see in the empirical section of the paper (Figures~\ref{fig:multi_exchange_amu_by_strike}--\ref{fig:multi_exchange_amu_by_tte}), $R$ is quite a natural proxy that can be used to characterize MU.Think of market unfairness through an imaginary maker who joins a quote only when the parity payoff is in her favor. It is a imaginary expectation, conditional on getting filled, not a realized profit, which depends on queue position, adverse selection and on a taker crossing. The compensation of the market maker in general is the bid--ask spread which already pays her for the usual costs of making a market---order processing, inventory, adverse selection, queue risk. Market unfairness is separate:it measures only how differently the two equivalent instruments---the real quote and its parity-implied synthetic---are priced against one another.

% \end{equation}
% where $S_{t}$ is the index level (so $m^{\mathrm{bp}}$ is in basis points of notional, the baseline used above) and $K_{i,t}=C^{\mathrm{mid}}_{i,t}+P^{\mathrm{mid}}_{i,t}$ is the \emph{option capital}, the sum of the call and put mid prices---the premium actually deployed in the two legs, so $m^{\mathrm{cap}}$ is in basis points of that capital, a return-on-capital view of the wedge. Each summary inherits the normalization as a superscript: $\mathrm{MMA}^{\bullet}$, $U_c^{\bullet}$ and $U_u^{\bullet}$ for $\bullet\in\{\$,\mathrm{bp},\mathrm{cap}\}$. The rate $R$ is unitless, and the decomposition $U_u^{\bullet}=R\,U_c^{\bullet}$ holds in every unit. Correspondingly we report the quoted put--call \emph{spread} in the same three units, $\mathrm{Spr}^{\$}$, $\mathrm{Spr}^{\mathrm{bp}}$ and $\mathrm{Spr}^{\mathrm{cap}}$ (dollars, and basis points of the underlying and of the option capital); unlike MMA and MU, the spread carries no cost term. Unless a superscript is shown, $\mathrm{MMA}$ and $U$ denote the basis-point-of-notional version.


\subsection{Description of arbitrage configurations}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\maybeincludegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{artifacts/arbitrage_paths_crossed_noncrossed.pdf}
\caption{Real quoted versus parity-implied (synthetic) option market. When the markets are crossed and disjoint ($C_{ask}<C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid}$, or the symmetric case), there is riskless arbitrage in the conventional sense. When one market is nested inside the other, or when the two straddle each other, there is no guaranteed profit but two paths carry a \emph{potential} market-maker advantage, realized only if the maker's quote is joined and then hit. In the nested case these are the backward join-call-bid and forward join-call-ask paths ($C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid}-C_{bid}$ and $C_{ask}-C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}$), with the join-put payoffs equal to their negatives (exactly so when the futures market has zero spread; the trading cost need not be zero); in the straddling case they are the backward join-call-bid and backward join-put-ask paths ($C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid}-C_{bid}$ and $C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}-C_{ask}$). The latter two configurations are what market unfairness aggregates. Schematic, illustrative levels.}
\label{fig:arb_paths_crossed_noncrossed}
\end{figure}

It is useful to see what market unfairness is measuring geometrically. For a given option leg---say the call---the quotes define a \emph{real} market $[C_{bid},C_{ask}]$, while parity combined with the put and the matching futures defines a \emph{synthetic} market $[C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid},C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}]$, where $C^{\mathrm{syn}}=P+(F-K)e^{-rT}$. Both intervals live on the same price axis, and their relative position determines whether, and how strongly, an arbitrage exists. Figure~\ref{fig:arb_paths_crossed_noncrossed} shows the three qualitatively distinct configurations.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\maybeincludegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{artifacts/backward_joincall_schematic.pdf}
\caption{One path in detail: the backward join-call-bid (illustrative, exaggerated). \emph{Left:} the synthetic call is assembled from parity---the call and put quotes and the forward-minus-strike $F-K$, giving $C^{\mathrm{syn}}=P+(F-K)e^{-rT}$; the object we measure is a top-of-book \emph{quote} (a bid/ask price), not a payoff. \emph{Right:} when the real call quote sits below its parity-implied synthetic quote $C^{\mathrm{syn}}=P+(F-K)e^{-rT}$, the maker \emph{joins}---rests a passive buy at the real call bid---and, once that resting order is hit, \emph{crosses} the synthetic market, selling the parity-equivalent synthetic at its bid. The arb is $C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{bid}-C_{bid}$, net of the round-trip cost (the nested backward join-call-bid of Figure~\ref{fig:arb_paths_crossed_noncrossed}). Only the resting leg is passive; the arb is therefore a payoff \emph{conditional on the maker being filled}, not a guaranteed trading profit. Schematic, illustrative levels.}
\label{fig:backward_joincall_schematic}
\end{figure}

MMA and MU do not measure the spread itself, which is the compensation makers demand for liquidity provision. They measure where the real and synthetic quotes are: each real quote must track its synthetic counterpart within the cost cushion (Section~\ref{sec:noamu-band}), and MU is what occurs outside that tolerance. This is how similarity with the NBBO rule arises: the synthetic market is the other market, and the market maker should not quote a worse price than what is available elsewhere (i.e in a synthetic way).

\subsection{The no-MU band and the shape of the MMA distribution}
\label{sec:noamu-band}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\maybeincludegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{artifacts/amu_fair_band_synthetic.pdf}
\caption{No $U_{u}$ requires each real quote to track its matching synthetic quote within $\pm\mathrm{cost}'=\pm(\mathrm{cost}+\frac{1}{2}(F_{ask}-F_{bid})e^{-rT})$. The band centers are the frictionless (no-cost) synthetic prices $P_{bid}+(F_{mid}-K)e^{-rT}$ and $P_{ask}+(F_{mid}-K)e^{-rT}$; the cushion around each is $\pm\mathrm{cost}'$. As frictions shrink, the real market is squeezed onto the synthetic market. Schematic, illustrative levels.}
\label{fig:amu_fair_band_synthetic}
\end{figure}

The configurations in Figure~\ref{fig:arb_paths_crossed_noncrossed} raise a sharper question: for a given synthetic market, how far can a real quote move before it becomes unfair? The answer pins each real quote to its synthetic counterpart within a tolerance set by the trading cost, and it fixes the shape that the MMA distribution ``should'' take.

For a detailed explanation, consider the real call ask $C_{ask}$. Two of the four paths in Table~\ref{tab:arbitrage_eight_formulas} contain it---backward/join-put-ask and forward/join-call-ask. Requiring both payoffs to be non-positive (no $U_{u}$) yields
\begin{equation}
P_{ask}+(F_{ask}-K)e^{-rT}-\mathrm{cost}
\;\le\; C_{ask} \;\le\;
P_{ask}+(F_{ask}-K)e^{-rT}+\mathrm{cost}.
\label{eq:cask-band}
\end{equation}
For later convenience let's define $\mathrm{cost}'\equiv\mathrm{cost}+\frac{1}{2}(F_{ask}-F_{bid})e^{-rT}$, which is the cost plus half the futures spread.  
Both bounds use the \emph{same-side} put quote $P_{ask}$, so the band is centred on the synthetic ask at the futures ask, $C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}\equiv P_{ask}+(F_{mid}-K)e^{-rT}$, with half-width $\mathrm{cost}'$. So a fair real market must track the synthetic market side by side, to within $\pm\mathrm{cost}'$ (Figure~\ref{fig:amu_fair_band_synthetic}). It is \emph{not} that $C_{ask}$ must lie inside the synthetic bid--ask up to some cost. Rather, $C_{ask}$ is tied to the synthetic \emph{ask} within the $\pm\mathrm{cost}$ cushion. Limits to the other quote-sides are analogous.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\maybeincludegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{artifacts/amu_schematic_mma_distribution.pdf}
\caption{Schematic MMA distribution. The dashed rectangle over $(-2\,\mathrm{cost}',0)$ is the no-MU band (uniform quoting), with $\mathrm{cost}'\equiv\mathrm{cost}+\tfrac12(F_{ask}-F_{bid})e^{-rT}$. Competitive quoting instead concentrates the mode at $-\mathrm{cost}'$, the symmetric equilibrium of the conjugate backward/forward paths; the mass that crosses zero into positive territory is the $U_{u}$. Note that this shape assumes a constant dollar cost, if cost scales with the index or the premium, skewed shapes may arise. Schematic, illustrative levels.}
\label{fig:amu_schematic_mma_distribution}
\end{figure}

Equation~\eqref{eq:cask-band} also fixes the shape of the MMA distribution. Write $u=C_{ask}-C^{\mathrm{syn}}_{ask}$; the two conjugate $C_{ask}$ payoffs are exactly $u-\mathrm{cost}'$ (backward, join put ask) and $-u-\mathrm{cost}'$ (forward, join call ask). As $C_{ask}$ ranges over its fair band $u\in(-\mathrm{cost}',\mathrm{cost}')$, each sweeps linearly over $(-2\,\mathrm{cost}',0)$. If makers quoted with no preferred point inside the band, the implied MMA would be \emph{uniform} on $(-2\,\mathrm{cost}',0)$: a rectangle of width $2\,\mathrm{cost}'$ just left of zero. That rectangle is the no-information benchmark (dashed in Figure~\ref{fig:amu_schematic_mma_distribution}).

The observed distribution instead peaks near $-\mathrm{cost}'$. Two things to point here. First just as $C_{ask}$ depends on $P_{ask}$ with a correction, $P_{ask}$ is limited by $C_{ask}$ with the same offset. Each unfair quote has a conjugate pair: if $C_{ask}$ is too rich, then $P_{ask}$ is too cheap by the same amound. Assembling tickpaths by conjugate pairs shows that the distribution of MMA must be symmetric around $\mathrm{cost}'$ 

Second, market makers have an interest in not posting negative MMA quotes. Heuristically, if we assume similar pressure on both conjugate paths to be more positive, an equilibrium would form where they are equally negative, and the distribution would peak at $-\mathrm{cost}'$. (Figures~\ref{fig:amu_schematic_mma_distribution} and~\ref{fig:all_markets_avg_4_spreads}). Stale or aggressive quotes populate the tails; the right tail crossing zero is the MU.

\section{Empirical structure of the MU}
\label{sec:descriptive}

\subsection{The shape of the MMA distribution and why it peaks below zero}
\label{sec:peak}

\lead{The MMA density peaks at minus the cost.} The most informative object in the data is the shape of the MMA distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:all_markets_avg_4_spreads}). Pooled across markets and paths, the density peaks sharply at minus the trading cost---near $-\CostBp$ basis points---and falls away on both sides. Zero splits the region where the maker cannot profit from the positive tail that feeds $U_{u}$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\maybeincludegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{artifacts/all_markets_avg_4_spreads.pdf}
\caption{Average density of the four MMA paths across all markets. The distribution peaks at minus the round-trip cost (dashed line); everything to the left of zero yields no maker profit, and only the positive tail to the right of zero contributes to $U_{u}$.}
\label{fig:all_markets_avg_4_spreads}
\end{figure}


\lead{The peak is where competition drove the wedge down to cost.} The reading is central to the paper. Makers improve quotes only as much as competition forces, so the mass sits at $-\text{cost}$: in the modal state the wedge is competed down to about the cost of the trade. Quoting is noisy, so the distribution is not a point mass. Quotes too good for the taker side fill the left tail; quotes too bad leave a positive wedge and fill the right tail. The right tail's conditional mean is the conditional unfairness, its share is $R$, and their product is the unconditional unfairness. Market unfairness is nonzero because in an imperfect market market makers do not always face enough competition. While some of this is idiosyncratic noise, we will see evidence that not all of it is.

The ratio of the unconditional to the conditional column recovers $R$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:amu-decomposition}).

\section{The 2024 regulatory shift}
\label{sec:regulation}

\lead{Regulation, not parity, changed in 2024.} Three milestones cluster in 2024. On January 10 the SEC approved spot Bitcoin ETPs, connecting crypto price risk to regulated custody, authorized-participant arbitrage, and mainstream brokerage \citep{sec_spot_bitcoin_etp_2024}. In May it approved spot Ether ETFs, which began trading in July \citep{sec_spot_ether_etp_2024}. In parallel, MiCA came into force in stages: stablecoin provisions in mid-2024, the CASP licensing regime at end-2024 \citep{esma_mica_2024}. None of this changed put--call parity; it changed who can hold, finance, and hedge crypto exposure at scale, and under what legal certainty.

\lead{Bigger, regulated players plausibly need a smaller premium.} This is where market unfairness is useful as a diagnostic. Whatever keeps a parity wedge open, it is plausibly cheaper to bear for a large, diversified, well-capitalized institution than for the crypto-native firms that dominated before: regulation eases custody and legal risk, balance-sheet depth eases funding risk, diversification eases inventory and convergence risk. If 2024 brought such participants into quoting these markets---directly, or by financing those who do---one would expect the wedge to shrink, beyond any gain in the observable frictions. This is a working hypothesis, not something we identify cleanly: we treat 2024 as a descriptive regime marker and ask whether the evidence fits.

\lead{This gives two descriptive questions.} Two descriptive questions follow: did $U_{u}$ drop around the 2024 changes, and does the drop line up with the observable frictions (spreads, depth, staleness), or persist beyond them? Sections~\ref{sec:rq2} and~\ref{sec:regressions} take these in turn.

\section{Regression analysis}
\label{sec:regressions}

\subsection{Empirical design}
\label{sec:design}

\lead{The outcome is cell-level unconditional unfairness.} The dependent variable in all four regressions is the cell-level unconditional unfairness, $U_{u,g,t}=R_{g,t}\,U_{c,g,t}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:amu-decomposition})---a quote-level, panel-observable object. Regressors enter in three blocks: the post-2024 indicator $\mathrm{Post}_t$; segment dummies (exchange, underlying, moneyness, maturity); and three liquidity frictions,
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Spr}_{g,t}&=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\texttt{mean\_call\_spread\_bp}_{g,t}+\texttt{mean\_put\_spread\_bp}_{g,t}\right),\\
\mathrm{Depth}_{g,t}&=\log\!\left(\texttt{mean\_min\_quote\_size\_dollar}_{g,t}\right),\\
\mathrm{Stale}_{g,t}&=\text{mean stale-quote fraction across call, put, and spot legs}.
\end{align}
\lead{Frictions enter as descriptors, not to identify a premium.} The direct-cost term is already subtracted inside every path payoff (Table~\ref{tab:arbitrage_eight_formulas}), so market unfairness is the wedge that survives above that floor (Eq.~\ref{eq:amu-identity}). We add the friction variables not to identify a structural premium but to describe: how much of $U_{u}$, and of its 2024 change, moves with observable book quality---wider spreads, thinner depth, staler quotes \citep{amihud1986asset, shleifer1997limits, garleanu2011margin, bollen2004modeling}---and how much does not.

\lead{The break date is January 10, 2024.} $\mathrm{Post}_t$ equals one on and after January 10, 2024---the Bitcoin ETP approval, first of the 2024 milestones---treated as a single regime date rather than separate shocks.

\lead{We run four nested specifications.} We estimate four specifications, adding regressors in blocks:
\begin{align}
U_{u,g,t}&=\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}\mathrm{Post}_t+\varepsilon_{g,t},\\
U_{u,g,t}&=\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}\mathrm{Post}_t+\gamma_g+\varepsilon_{g,t},\\
U_{u,g,t}&=\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}\mathrm{Post}_t+\beta_{\mathrm{OKX}}\mathrm{OKX}_g+\beta_{\mathrm{ETH}}\mathrm{ETH}_g+\beta_{\mathrm{ATM}}\mathrm{ATM}_g+\beta_{\mathrm{NE}}\mathrm{NearExp}_g+\varepsilon_{g,t},\\
U_{u,g,t}&=\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}\mathrm{Post}_t+\beta_{\mathrm{Depth}}\mathrm{Depth}_{g,t}+\beta_{\mathrm{Spr}}\mathrm{Spr}_{g,t}+\beta_{\mathrm{Stale}}\mathrm{Stale}_{g,t}+\gamma_g+\varepsilon_{g,t},
\end{align}
all with a constant and \RegSEdesc{} standard errors. Specification (1) is the raw regime effect. Specification (2) adds cell fixed effects $\gamma_g$, isolating the within-cell shift. Specification (3) instead adds the segment dummies as pooled OLS: there the dummies are the objects of interest (the cross-sectional question), so we estimate rather than absorb them. Specification (4) adds the frictions with cell fixed effects. We prefer cell fixed effects because the surface dependence is strongly nonlinear (Figures~\ref{fig:multi_exchange_amu_by_strike}--\ref{fig:multi_exchange_amu_by_tte}); linear dummies would leave segment structure in the residual. In (4), $\beta_{\mathrm{Post}}$ is the within-cell change net of both the frictions and the full segment structure. Table~\ref{tab:regressor_abbreviations} defines the abbreviations.

\subsection{Results}

\lead{We use the regressions to check that market unfairness behaves like a market-quality statistic.} We do not try to identify why $U_{u}$ fell. We ask whether it behaves as a good market-quality measure should. Two things are required: it should fall when the market is independently believed to have improved (the 2024 institutionalization), and that fall should not be an artifact of which contracts trade. Table~\ref{tab:regression_coefficients} checks both. The columns are (1) the raw post-2024 shift; (2) plus cell fixed effects; (3) segment dummies (pooled OLS); (4) all three frictions with cell fixed effects; and (5)--(7) each friction on its own, with cell fixed effects. Each cell stacks three rows: the coefficient (with significance stars), the \RegSEdesc{} standard error in parentheses, and, in bold brackets, the standardized effect $\beta\,\mathrm{std}(X)/\mathrm{std}(Y)$.

\lead{$U_{u}$ is markedly lower after 2024.} \emph{Specification (1).} The post-2024 coefficient is negative and large, and stays highly significant under two-way clustered errors. $U_{u}$ is much lower after 2024---the period the market is widely agreed to have improved. This is the first thing a market-quality measure should show, and addresses the second question: $U_{u}$ did drop upon the 2024 regime shift.

\lead{The drop is within cells, not a change in contract mix.} \emph{Specification (2).} Adding cell fixed effects barely moves the post-2024 coefficient. The drop happens \emph{within} fixed strike--maturity--venue cells over time, not because the traded mix shifted. This rules out the main composition artifact---the second thing we need.

\lead{$U_{u}$ differs across segments, but only slightly.} \emph{Specification (3).} With segment dummies (pooled OLS), $U_{u}$ differs by exchange and moneyness---both strongly significant even under clustered errors---while the underlying (ETH) and near-expiry effects are marginal or insignificant once errors are clustered. All are small next to variation over the surface. The low $R^2$ is expected: linear dummies cannot capture the nonlinear surface, which is why (4) uses fixed effects. This addresses the first question: $U_{u}$ differs across venues and underlyings, but only modestly.

\lead{The three frictions together explain the drop.} \emph{Specification (4).} We add spread, depth, and staleness, still with cell fixed effects. All three are strong under two-way clustered errors (by cell and by day). Together they pull the post-2024 coefficient down to about zero, and it is no longer significant. So on the filtered panel these three book-quality measures fully account for the within-cell drop.

\lead{No single friction does it on its own.} \emph{Specifications (5)--(7).} Here we add the frictions one at a time. With depth alone (5), or staleness alone (7), the post-2024 coefficient barely moves and stays large and significant. With spread alone (6) it falls a lot but is still significant. Only all three together, in (4), bring it to zero. So the drop is a mix of the three frictions, and spread does most of the work.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}

\lead{Standard errors are clustered, not treated as independent.} The regression uses one observation per cell-day, but these are not independent draws. A cell---a fixed strike/maturity/venue bucket---is observed on many days and its wedge is persistent, so its residuals are correlated over time; and all cells share market-wide conditions on a given day, so residuals are correlated across cells within a date. Heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) errors assume independence across cell-days and are, in consequence, far too small: with hundreds of cells observed over hundreds of days, the effective amount of independent information is closer to the number of cells or days than to the number of cell-days. We therefore report \RegSEdesc{} standard errors, clustering two ways---by cell and by day \citep{cameron2011robust}. Only the covariance changes; the coefficients are identical. The mechanism is that the clustered variance sums each cluster's aggregated score \emph{before} squaring it, so positively correlated within-cluster residuals no longer cancel. In our panel the clustered errors are several times larger than HC1; that is what the reported standard errors and significance stars reflect. The 2024 compression and the friction gradients remain significant under this more conservative variance, but their $t$-statistics are far smaller than a naive HC1 calculation would suggest.

\lead{The friction controls enter jointly, and collinearity does not distort the regime effect.} Spread, depth, and staleness are strongly correlated---a thin book tends to be wide, shallow, and slow to update at once. Collinearity of this kind inflates the variances of the \emph{individual} friction coefficients and makes them hard to read one by one, but it does not bias the coefficient of interest, the post-2024 indicator, which is identified off variation orthogonal to the frictions. We verified this with a principal-component check: because the principal components are an orthogonal rotation of the three standardized frictions, entering all three components reproduces specification (4) exactly, and any full-rank recombination of the frictions leaves the post-2024 coefficient unchanged. (A single component is not a substitute: keeping only the first principal component is a rank-one control that discards the other two friction directions---one of which is strongly related to $U_{u}$ despite carrying little of the frictions' variance---so it merely under-controls and inflates the apparent regime effect.) We therefore report the three frictions jointly in specification (4).

\lead{The results do not hinge on the cost or the discount rate.} Two auxiliary parameters enter the construction: the round-trip cost (baseline \CostBp~bp) and the discount rate (baseline $r=5\%$). Neither is crucial. The cost only sets the scale of market unfairness---a fixed proportional cost shifts the level, but the pre/post \emph{difference} in unconditional unfairness barely moves with it (the conditional form is more cost-sensitive, one reason the headline uses the unconditional measure), and a common cost keeps cross-venue comparisons coherent. The discount rate matters even less, for a structural reason: $r$ enters only through $e^{-rT}$, which multiplies a small quantity---the futures leg $(F-K)$ in each payoff and the futures half-spread $\tfrac12(F_{ask}-F_{bid})$ in $\mathrm{cost}'$. Its effect on a payoff is $\partial\!\left[(F-K)e^{-rT}\right]/\partial r=-T(F-K)e^{-rT}$, of order $T\,(F-K)/\mathrm{index}$; since our strikes sit within $\pm 21\%$ of the forward and most observation mass sits in near-the-money, short-dated cells where $(F-K)$ and $T$ are smallest, plausible changes in $r$ move $U_{u}$ negligibly---and the pre/post difference more negligibly still, since $r$ shifts both regimes almost identically. Evaluating $U_{u}$ across grids of both parameters---the cost grid read off the same filtered panel as the regressions, the discount-rate grid recomputed at each $r$ (Table~\ref{tab:cost_r_sensitivity})---bears this out. A higher assumed cost lowers the $U_{u}$ level and shrinks the compression somewhat, but the pre/post drop stays large and negative across the whole cost grid; across $r$ it is essentially constant. Under every choice the 2024 compression is present and sizable.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

\lead{$U_{u}$ is a model-free symptom of imperfect quoting.} We propose \emph{Absolute Market Unfairness}: the cost-adjusted put--call-parity violation left in the standing quotes of inverse cryptocurrency option markets. It is model-free and comparable in basis points---a transparent sign that the top of the book is not where frictionless parity would put it---not a full account of a venue's liquidity or a cleanly identified risk premium. It says nothing about fundamental mispricing and guarantees no dollar profit.

\lead{$U_{u}$ is small across venues, shaped by the surface, and fell in 2024.} Using Deribit and OKX BTCUSD and ETHUSD options from 2020 through June 2026: $U_{u}$ differs across venues and underlyings, but little next to its variation over the surface, where it is governed mainly by how often a wedge appears---smallest for month-scale, near-the-money contracts, largest in the illiquid corners. It fell sharply over 2023--2024, around the ETF and MiCA dates---though much of the decline was already under way before them---and the compression is present at every cost level we examine; most of the drop survives controls for spreads, depth, and staleness within cells. This is consistent with the entry of participants whose quoting improved market quality, not just more passive depth---though the design is descriptive and does not identify the mechanism. The value of market unfairness is that it turns a qualitative worry about quote quality into a model-free number, comparable across venues, underlyings, and maturities, and trackable through regulatory change.

\lead{The three questions, answered.} \emph{How large is $U_{u}$, and does it differ across exchanges and underlyings?} It is small---about \AmuMeanBp~bp of notional on average (\AmuPreBp~bp before 2024, \AmuPostBp~bp after)---and it differs across venues and underlyings only modestly; far more of its variation comes from position on the option surface. \emph{Did $U_{u}$ drop upon the 2024 regulatory and institutional regime shift?} Yes: it fell sharply over 2023--2024 around the ETF and MiCA dates (much of the decline preceding them), the drop is highly significant under standard errors clustered by cell and day, and it is present at every trading cost and discount rate we examine. \emph{Is the drop fully explained by the usual liquidity frictions, or does it partly reflect a new class of parity-enforcing participants?} It is not fully explained by spreads, depth, and staleness: a substantial within-cell drop survives them. This is consistent with---though our descriptive design does not identify---the entry of participants whose quoting improved market quality rather than merely adding passive depth.
